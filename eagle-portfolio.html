01 — Overview

What exactly is an eagle?

Eagles are large birds of prey defined by powerful builds, broad wings, and hooked beaks built for tearing flesh. They occupy the very top of the food chain in nearly every habitat they inhabit — forests, mountains, wetlands, and coastlines alike.

02 — Anatomy

Built like a living weapon

Every part of an eagle's body is refined for aerial dominance. Talons can clamp down with roughly ten times the grip force of a human hand, locking onto prey mid-strike without a second thought.

The hooked beak is not used to kill — it's a precision tool for tearing meat, feathers, and hide once prey has already been subdued by the feet.

Beneath the plumage, a keel-shaped breastbone anchors flight muscles that make up nearly a third of total body weight, giving eagles the power to carry prey heavier than themselves.

The hooked beak and piercing stare — built for precision, not force
03 — The Eye

Vision sharper than any lens

An eagle's eye is nearly the same size as a human's, yet it delivers four to eight times the visual acuity. A dense concentration of light-sensitive cells and a deep central fovea let it resolve fine detail across immense distances.

A nictitating membrane — a translucent third eyelid — sweeps across the eye to clear debris mid-flight without ever losing sight of the target below.

04 — Signature Traits

Five things that define an eagle

Telescopic Eyesight

Can spot a moving hare from over three kilometres away while circling on a thermal current.

Broad Soaring Wings

Long, deeply fingered flight feathers reduce drag, letting eagles glide for hours on rising air with barely a wingbeat.

Vice-Grip Talons

Four curved talons per foot lock into a fixed grip strong enough to crush bone on impact.

Lifelong Pair Bonds

Most species mate for life, returning to the same nesting territory year after year.

Fortress Nests

Eyries built from branches are reused and expanded annually, some growing to over a tonne in weight.

Explosive Dives

Species like the golden eagle exceed 240 km/h in a full hunting dive — among the fastest animals alive.

05 — Species Spotlight

Three eagles, three continents

Of the roughly 60 recognised species, these three are the most iconic representatives of the family — each shaped by a very different home range.

North America

Bald Eagle

Haliaeetus leucocephalus. A fish-hunting specialist and the national bird of the United States, recognised instantly by its white head and tail.

Coastal Alaska

Sea Eagle

Haliaeetus species thrive along coastlines and river deltas, snatching salmon straight from the water's surface in a low, fast pass.

Northern Wetlands

Golden Eagle Kin

Found across Eurasia and North America's mountains, this highland hunter takes prey as large as young deer in open, treeless terrain.

06 — Life Cycle

From hatchling to apex hunter

I

Egg & Incubation

1–3 eggs laid per clutch, incubated for roughly 35 days by both parents in shifts.

II

Nestling

Chicks are fed torn scraps of meat by parents and grow flight feathers over 10–12 weeks.

III

Fledgling

First flights are clumsy and short-range; young eagles practise landings for several weeks nearby.

IV

Maturity

Full adult plumage and breeding readiness typically arrive between four and six years of age.

● Recovering — Least Concern (most species)

Once endangered, now a conservation success story

Pesticide contamination and habitat loss pushed several eagle species to the brink in the 20th century. Legal protection, breeding programmes, and banning harmful pesticides brought many populations, including the bald eagle, back from near-extinction over the following decades.
